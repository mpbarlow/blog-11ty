WWDC 2026

8th Jun 2026 • 2 minute read

Tags: ai tech

I appreciated that they led with addressing some of the biggest concerns, especially from long-time Mac users. The changes to Liquid Glass are very welcome: real toolbars, sane sidebars (saner—they’re still full height which is not great), and thank whoever you can for tighter corner radii. I had assumed that was an affordance for future touch Macs and that we’d be stuck with it forever, but mercifully that’s not the case.

The visual changes look like a big step in the right direction, with legibility of text explicitly called out, and the edges of controls noticeably more defined. Not only is this a legibility improvement but I just think it plain looks better too. Is it a good design now? No, I don’t think so—certainly not like we had fifteen years ago—and I think the concession of a translucency slider lends some evidence to that. But what shipped in Tahoe was very poor, so I’ll welcome any and all improvements with open arms. I am cautiously optimistic.

The note on improved performance was interesting. Of course, with only percentage improvements given and no actual substantive measurements it doesn’t mean a whole lot, but I’ll take it. I wonder if the improvements were made with the MacBook Neo in mind?

I don’t have kids, so I have very little to say on the middle section. I’ve never really used any parental controls and probably never will, but I hear from friends who do have kids that they’re incredibly unreliable and often forget settings. Here’s hoping these changes improve that. It also feels like this section was aimed more at regulators than parents. That’s probably no coincidence, with just today our soggy cardboard Prime Minister demanding that Apple and Google block nudity on phones for under 18s. That will surely have no negative ramifications at all.

The AI section was odd. I think the sort of stuff we used to call “machine learning” is the only stuff under the AI umbrella that’s actually useful. The sort of stuff that makes an existing app better rather than pretending to be a product in its own right, and coincidentally tends to have the fewest moral quandaries attached. It often runs on-device and at least doesn’t have to have been trained via widespread ransacking of the commons (though might well have). They made a big deal about finding use cases that work for the user, and the general “find and surface stuff on my phone” leaned heavy into that.

But then they went full chatbot, full refresh of that godforsaken Image Playgrounds app, and full discouragement of engaging one’s neurons. “Do these boots fit in this bag?” we are told to ask, looking at the boots and the bag on the table in front of us. What lofty heights technology has brought us to.

At least they fixed the corner radii.
